AI literacy primer for staff: how it is being made (draft for review)

This page describes the process, not the content. The primer is a plain-language guide for SCII staff, built to help anyone, whatever their subject or starting point, to understand the main ideas, judge claims, tools and sources for themselves, see where their own subject differs, treat contested ideas as questions to argue with, tell what is stable from what is changing, and keep talking about it as the debate moves. It is a draft for review, not approved, and not for circulation.

Principle: we always make complexity simple a step above, and we stop at three layers: a plain summary, a fuller explanation, then the source. Beyond that we link out, so the depth is never bottomless.

Process steps

Anyone in the group, whatever their role, can pick this up and run it again, for a new topic, a new subject area or the next review. It does not depend on one person.

  1. Agree the purpose. Say who it is for and what they should be able to do afterwards. Check with the SCII Gen AI group what "baseline" should mean, so the work is right first time.
  2. List the questions. Write down the questions the audience needs answered, not the answers.
  3. Gather and check sources. For each question, find sources and add each to a register before using it: its type, whose interest it serves, and how far to lean on it.
  4. Map the current dialogue. For each subject area, identify what is being said now, by whom, and where views differ. Date each snapshot.
  5. Draft in layers. Write in plain language, from basic ideas to current debates, with numbered references so every claim can be traced.
  6. Develop with each subject area. Subject leads correct and extend their part, since each subject brings its own opportunities and challenges.
  7. Review by the group. Set a review window and give reviewers clear questions about what to cut, change or add.
  8. Revise and log. Update the draft, record what was decided and why, and put a review date and a named owner on each page.
  9. Build and check accessibility. Produce the web and printable outputs from the one source, built and checked to WCAG AA, with a tagged PDF as the accessible fallback.
  10. Approve, hand over and keep talking. Agree approval, a home on SHU infrastructure and board hand-off. Confirm the owner, where the files live and when the next conversation happens. Each subject area keeps an ongoing conversation with subject leads, staff and students about what has changed, and what it learns feeds the next review. The conversation runs to a set rhythm (for example once a term) with a named owner, and at each review the group decides whether to continue, change or stop.

Indicative headings (to be shaped with the group)

These are signposts for what the primer would cover, not its content. Headings can change as the group and subject leads shape it.

Each heading has a short sample. Samples are illustrations of the level of detail, not final wording.

Main branches

Read the draft primer: AI literacy primer for staff (draft for review). It is a draft, not approved, and is the fuller version of the branches below.

  • The basics

    Sample. Machine learning: the system learns patterns from examples instead of following written rules, so nobody writes the rules it ends up using.

  • How generative AI works

    Sample. Token: the small chunk of text (a word or part of a word) that a language model reads and writes. Limits and costs come from the model working in tokens rather than letters.

  • Behaviour and limits

    Sample. Hallucination: fluent output that is false or unsupported, including invented references. A confident tone is not evidence.

  • Using AI in practice

    Sample. Disclosure: saying when and how AI was used. It is about transparency and trust as well as rule-following.

  • Contemporary themes

    Sample. Assessment validity: the question is no longer only "was AI used?" but whether assessment still shows that the student met the standard.

  • Ways of thinking

    Sample. What counts as taking? Imitation, quotation, sampling, pastiche and plagiarism sit on a spectrum, and law, ethics and academic integrity draw the lines in different places. Where does influence end and theft begin?

  • Current models (dated, reviewed each term)

    Sample. What does not change with versions: it predicts plausible output rather than checking truth, and responsibility for the work stays with the person who submits it.

  • AI and neurodivergence

    Sample. Assessment rules can remove existing support: a blanket "no AI" rule can take away tools students already rely on.

  • By subject area

    Sample. Each subject brings its own opportunities and challenges, so each has its own branch (below).

Subject branches (starting with creative writing, fine art, fashion, graphic design, architecture and games design; others to be added with their leads). Samples here are from creative writing.

  • Opportunities

    Sample. Help with ideas, structure and editing, especially when guided on when and how to use the tool.

  • Challenges

    Sample. In one experiment with 225 students, AI raised performance but diminished students' experience of their own creativity.

  • Current dialogue

    Sample. In a 2024 Society of Authors survey, 86% of respondents were concerned that generative AI devalues human-made creative work.

  • Questions to ask

    Sample. What is the learning in the blank page, and which uses of AI protect it?

What you need

  • A named owner and a few reviewers, including at least one subject lead.
  • A shared folder, with one plain-text source file as the master and a log of decisions.
  • A source register, and a fixed review date.
  • A way to run the accessibility check.

Check it worked

  • Someone who did not write it can follow the log and reproduce the outputs from the source.
  • Every claim traces to a source in the register.
  • The next review date and owner are on every page.

Rules and changes

This draft sets the rules for the project: the principle, the process steps, the headings and the limits on depth and time. Unless something is agreed otherwise, the project follows it.

When something is agreed otherwise:

  1. Record the change in the development log: what changed, why, who agreed, and the date.
  2. Update this page to match, so it always shows the current rules.
  3. Rebuild the published page from the source, and set the updated date.

This primer is for SCII staff, whatever your subject or starting point. 01 The basics

Artificial intelligence (AI)

Software that performs tasks we normally associate with human intelligence, such as recognising, predicting, translating or recommending. Once something works reliably we stop calling it AI (spell-check).

In Everyday Use

In current news, "AI" usually means generative AI, but the term covers much older tools too. [7] Examples range from a spell-checker or route planner to a model that writes an essay.

What to Ask

They work differently and fail differently, so "AI" alone does not tell you what to expect or what to check. Useful questions: what does it take in, what does it produce, and who checks the result?

Further Reading

  • Jisc, A generative AI primer A plain-language overview for UK education, regularly updated.
Algorithm

A fixed, step-by-step procedure for getting a result. In the news "the algorithm" often means any automated system that ranks, recommends or decides, including learned models, so ask which kind is meant.

Under the Bonnet

In machine learning, a learning algorithm is the procedure that adjusts the model; the model is what it produces.

A Quick Example

A recipe is an algorithm; so is a rule that sorts files by date. A recommendation feed combines fixed steps with learned scores, so "the algorithm decided" needs unpacking: who set the goal, what data it used, and who can change it?

Rule-based automation

People write the rules ("if invoice over £10,000, send for approval"). Predictable and auditable, but breaks on cases nobody anticipated.

How It Fails

A useful contrast: a rule-based system fails in ways someone can list; a learned system can fail in ways nobody listed.

A Quick Example

A form that rejects any date in the past.

Why It Still Matters

Rule-based systems still do much of the automation in organisations. Their advantage for assessment and compliance is that you can show exactly why a decision was made, which a learned system often cannot. [99]

Machine learning

The system learns patterns from examples instead of following written rules.

Under the Bonnet

In practice that means adjusting a very large set of numbers until the outputs fit the examples. Nobody writes the rules it ends up using, and they cannot simply be read off. The output of that learning is a model.

A Quick Example

Show a system thousands of labelled photos of cats and dogs and it adjusts until its guesses match the labels, then guesses on photos it has not seen. It can pick up shortcuts: if every cat photo was taken indoors, it may learn "indoors" instead of "cat". Its performance depends on what the examples covered. [100]

Neural network and deep learning

A neural network is layers of simple numerical units, loosely inspired by the brain, whose connection strengths are the model's parameters. Deep learning means many layers. Today's language and image models are built this way, which is why they are hard to inspect.

Under the Bonnet

Each unit takes numbers in, weights them and passes a number on; no single unit "knows" anything, and meaning sits in the pattern across many. Early layers tend to respond to simple features and later layers to combinations of them, but nobody sets those roles by hand, so explaining why a network gave a particular answer is only partly possible. [99]

Model (parameters, weights)

The product of training: a very large set of numbers that encodes patterns found in the training data. It is not a searchable store of the original material, though it can sometimes reproduce parts of it. [67][68]

Under the Bonnet

Each connection in the network has one number, a weight; the model is those weights plus the layout of the network. There is no lookup table to search, so "where did it get that?" has no direct answer, which is part of why provenance and copyright questions are hard.

What It Does Not Do

The weights are fixed once training ends: the model does not learn from your conversation unless a product stores it and feeds it back in. [src?]

Training data

The examples a model learned from, which may include text, images, code and audio, some licensed and some collected from the web. Its coverage, gaps and biases shape what the model can do, and whether the people who made the material consented to its use is a live question. [8][2]

A Quick Example

A text model may have read books, websites, forums and code; an image model, captioned pictures. Where a language, group or style is thin in the data, the model is usually weaker or more stereotyped there.

Why It Matters

Web-collected data can include personal data and copyrighted work, which is why training data is a policy question as well as a technical one. [2][26]

Training and inference

Training builds the model once, at great cost; inference is each time someone uses it. Both use energy, and the balance between them shapes the environmental picture. [9]

Under the Bonnet

In training the model predicts, is compared with the real answer, and has its weights adjusted, repeated an enormous number of times. Inference only reads the weights; it does not learn. A reply is produced one token at a time, so a long answer means many steps.

Chatbot, model and product

The model is the engine; the chatbot is the interface you type into; the product wraps both with extra instructions, search, memory, file upload and its own data terms. The name on the app is often not the name of the model, and the same model can behave differently in different products. Data handling and approval belong to the product, so check those at that level.

A Quick Example

The same model can sit behind a general chatbot, a coding assistant and a university-licensed tool, each with different instructions, filters and data terms.

What to Ask

Ask three things: which model, which product wraps it, and under whose data agreement.

02 How generative AI works

Generative AI

Models that produce new text, images, audio, video or code, rather than only classifying or predicting. The output is usually newly assembled from patterns in the training data rather than retrieved from a stored copy, and quality varies a lot by task. [7]

A Quick Example

Asked for a poster it assembles an image that fits the prompt; asked again, it makes a different one. Because it generates rather than retrieves, it can produce confident detail that is wrong or invented.

What to Do

Treat output as a draft to check; "new" means a new arrangement of learned patterns, not necessarily original thought.

Large language model (LLM)

A model trained on very large amounts of text to predict what comes next. Later training on instructions and human feedback shapes it to follow requests and sound helpful. It produces plausible language, not checked facts. [11][76]

Under the Bonnet

It splits text into tokens and, at each position, scores which token could come next, picks one, and repeats. Fluency comes from this process, not from looking facts up, which is why it can write well about things that are not true. [12][72]

Further Reading

  • Jisc, A generative AI primer A plain-language overview for UK education, regularly updated.
Foundation, frontier and open-weight models

A foundation model is a large general model that many products are built on; frontier means the most capable models at a given time; open-weight means the trained model files are published so others can run and adapt them. Open-weight is not the same as open source, because training data and code are often not released. [74][75]

Why It Matters

Because one foundation model can sit behind many apps, a flaw or bias in it can appear in all of them. Open-weight lets an institution run a model on its own machines, which helps with data control, but it then carries the work of checking and updating it.

Token

The small chunk of text (a word or part of a word) that a language model reads and writes. Limits and costs are counted in tokens.

Where It Shows Up

Miscounting letters in a word is linked in part to the model working in tokens rather than letters, though researchers also point to the difficulty of counting itself and to character-level gaps in training. [77] "unbelievable" might be split into three tokens, while a common short word is one.

Rules of Thumb

A rough rule for English is that a token is about three quarters of a word, so 1,000 words is nearer 1,300 tokens. Text in other languages and unusual spellings often take more tokens, so cost and limits can differ by language. [102][103]

Prompt and context window

The instructions you give, and how much text the model can consider at once. Anything outside the window is not seen, so long conversations can drift or lose early instructions.

What Goes in the Window

What you paste in is processed by the provider: see personal data below. The window holds your prompt, the earlier conversation, any pasted documents and the model's own replies.

When It Fills Up

When it fills, older material may no longer be taken into account, and even inside the window models use information in the middle of a long input less reliably than at the start or end. [101] For long tasks, restate key instructions and check what the tool still has in view.

System prompt

Instructions set by the provider or developer that the model reads before your message, covering tone, rules and what to refuse. They are usually hidden and can change without notice, which is one reason the same underlying model behaves differently across tools.

A Quick Example

[src?] A university tool may be told to stay on topic, decline some requests and add a disclaimer. You usually cannot see this, so you may not know why a tool refused or hedged. Two products on the same model can therefore differ in tone, caution and what they will do.

A Worked Example

Prompts can carry a company's choices on contested topics. In May 2025 xAI said an unauthorised change to Grok's prompt "directed Grok to provide a specific response on a political topic", and it then pledged to publish its prompts. [110][111] A New York Times analysis, as reported, found that prompt changes shifted Grok's answers on political questions to the right, although xAI describes Grok as politically neutral. [108] In July 2025 xAI said that relying on its developers' views was not the policy it wanted and that a fix to the underlying model was in the works. [109] A different kind of case: in April 2025 a ChatGPT update made GPT-4o noticeably flattering and OpenAI rolled it back from 28 April. OpenAI attributed it mainly to training changes that looked helpful separately but together likely weakened a check on flattery, and it used a system prompt update only as a quick mitigation before the rollback. [125][126] That was an unintended side effect, not an unauthorised or political change, so the two cases are not equivalent. A prompt can change behaviour fast, but it does not repair what training produced. These are documented cases from two companies, not a verdict on all vendors. Every chatbot has a system prompt, and training also sets values, so a published prompt is not the whole story. [112]

Diffusion model

The usual approach for image generation: it refines random noise into an image that fits the prompt. The same prompt gives a different image each time, and arguments about imitating artists' styles and training on their work are most visible here. [2]

Under the Bonnet

In training the model learns to remove noise from images that were deliberately blurred; to generate, it starts from noise and removes it step by step, steered by the text prompt. [106]

Multimodal

Handles more than one kind of input or output, such as text and images together, so a tool can describe an image, or generate one from a sketch and a sentence.

A Quick Example

Upload a chart and ask what trend it shows.

What Goes Wrong

Each kind of input adds failure modes, such as misreading small text in an image, and privacy issues, such as faces or location data in photos.

Fine-tuning and retrieval

Two ways of adapting a general model. Fine-tuning trains it further on specialist examples; retrieval gives it documents to draw on when answering. Retrieval can reduce errors but does not remove them. [71][72]

A Quick Example

Retrieval lets a tool answer from your course handbook without retraining; fine-tuning might teach it a house style. Retrieval is easier to update and to check, because you can see what it drew on, but the model can still misread or ignore it.

What to Ask

Ask whether a tool is answering from documents, from training, or both. [72]

Reasoning models

Models set up to produce intermediate steps before answering, spending more computation per question. They are usually slower and often better at maths, code and multi-step problems. The visible "thinking" is generated text, not a faithful record of how the answer was reached. [70]

A Quick Example

On a multi-step maths problem a reasoning model writes out steps before the answer, which often improves the result but takes longer and uses more tokens. The steps can look convincing and still contain errors, so read them as a draft, not as proof. [70]

How behaviour is shaped (human feedback, alignment, guardrails)

After training on text, models are trained further using human ratings and written rules so they follow requests and decline some of them, and tools add filters on top. This makes them more useful and safer, but not neutral: it shapes tone, refusals and a tendency to please (see agreeing too readily). Vendors differ in how they do it. [76][69]

Under the Bonnet

In outline, people rate or compare answers and the model is adjusted towards the ones they prefer, with written principles or rules added by the developer. The raters, their instructions and the company's priorities all influence what the model says and refuses, and because agreeable answers tend to be rated highly, the process can add to flattery. [69]

Tools and agents (short version)

On its own a model only produces text, images or code. Connecting it to tools such as web search, file reading or running code lets it take actions, and an agent chains those steps toward a goal. The full entry is under Using AI in practice.

A Quick Example

A tool that searches the web, reads three pages and drafts a summary is taking several steps on its own. Each step can go wrong and later steps build on earlier mistakes, so ask what it can read, what it can change and where a person checks.

03 Behaviour and limits

Probabilistic output

The model assigns a probability to each possible next word and the tool samples from those, rather than always taking the top choice. So the same prompt can give different answers, and a good result once is not a guarantee. Updates, hidden system prompts and earlier conversation also change results. For assessment, a marker cannot reproduce what a student's tool produced. [105]

A Quick Example

Ask for ten taglines and you get ten different ones; ask again tomorrow and you get more. Variation is a feature for brainstorming and a problem for anything that needs to be repeatable.

Hallucination

Fluent output that is false or unsupported, including invented references, quotations and dates. There is no intent to deceive, and a confident tone is not evidence. Risk is higher where the topic is obscure or the request asks for specific sources. [12][72]

A Quick Example

Asked for five journal articles on a topic, a tool may give five plausible titles, authors and journals, some of which do not exist. It happens because the tool produces what looks like a reference, not because it looked one up. Search-connected tools lower the rate but can still cite a real page for a claim it does not make. [72]

Bias

Patterns in training data can be reproduced or amplified in outputs, for example stereotyped images, uneven performance across languages and dialects, or a narrow idea of the "default" user. Detection tools can show it too. [8][11][25]

A Quick Example

Asked for an image of "a lecturer" or "a designer", a tool may default to one gender, age or ethnicity, and a tool tuned on mostly English text may work less well in other languages or dialects. The bias comes from what was in the data, how it was labelled and what people rated highly.

What to Ask

Ask who is missing from the output as well as who is shown.

Verification

Checking claims against sources you trust. Check that any cited source exists and says what is claimed; check images and quotations for provenance. It is the user's job, not the tool's.

A Practical Routine

Find the source yourself (do not ask the tool to confirm itself), open it, check it says what is claimed, and check the date. For images, look for where they came from. For numbers, recompute them. Record what you checked, because in assessment and in published work the person remains responsible for the result.

AI detectors

Tools that claim to flag AI-written text. Jisc advises that none can conclusively prove AI authorship, all give false positives, and they are easy to defeat; one study found them wrongly flagging non-native English writing. Treat a score as a prompt for a conversation, not as evidence. [24][25]

A Quick Example

A score of "60% likely AI" is a statistical guess about style, not a finding about what a person did, and the same text can score differently on another day or tool. Plain, regular or formulaic writing can look machine-like.

What to Do Instead

If a concern arises, ask about process (notes, drafts, sources, how the student reached the argument) before reaching for a score.

Agreeing too readily (sycophancy)

Tools tend to go along with the framing of a question and to praise the user's work, so a leading prompt can get a confirming answer. Ask for counter-arguments and test answers against your own judgement. [69]

A Quick Example

A student asks, "This essay is excellent, isn't it?" and gets agreement; asked, "What are the three weakest points?" the same tool finds real problems.

What to Do

Phrase prompts neutrally, ask for the strongest objection, and be wary of tools that always find your work good. It is a particular risk when students use AI for feedback.

Knowledge cutoff and currency

A model knows only what was in its training data up to a certain date. Tools that search the web can add current information, but quality varies, so check dates and sources.

A Quick Example

A model may describe a policy or a software version as current when it has since changed. Search-connected tools reduce this, but only if they search, and they may rely on poor pages.

What to Do

For anything that can change (law, prices, who holds a role, versions), check a dated primary source.

Explanations are not readouts

If you ask a model why it gave an answer, the reply is another generated text, not a record of how it actually worked. Treat self-explanations with the same care as any other output. [70]

A Quick Example

Asked "why did you mark this essay B?", the tool writes a reasonable-sounding rationale, but it may not describe what shaped the grade, and asked again it may give a different reason. Research on step-by-step reasoning found it can leave out what actually influenced the answer.

Why It Matters

For marking and feedback, the accountable reasoning has to be a person's. [70]

04 Using AI in practice

AI-assisted vs AI-generated

How much of the thinking and the work is yours. The range runs from brainstorming and editing, through drafting with heavy revision, to submitting generated work unchanged. The difference matters for authorship, assessment and credit, and for who is accountable for the result.

A Quick Example

Spell-check; asking for ideas; asking for a structure that you then rewrite; asking for a draft you edit lightly; submitting output unchanged.

Why It Matters

Where a task sits on this line decides what can be claimed as your own work and what needs saying. Disclosure and assessment briefs work best when they name what is and is not acceptable at each point.

Disclosure

Saying when and how AI was used. It is about transparency and trust as well as rule-following. Expectations differ between modules, so clarity matters more than the rule itself. [3][5]

A Quick Example

A short statement saying which tool was used, for what (brainstorming, editing, translation), what was kept, and what the person checked.

Making It Work

Disclosure is easier to ask for when it is routine and non-punitive, and easier to give when it is clear what counts as use. It supports assessment and academic integrity without relying on detection.

Supported tools and data boundary

Use institutional accounts for university work, so prompts stay inside the university's data protection arrangements. (Copilot is the SHU supported tool; see the Copilot Susceptibility Test Protocol.) Free consumer tools may handle inputs differently depending on their settings and terms, so check before use.

A Quick Example

The same prompt in a university-licensed tool and in a free consumer account may be stored, used and shared under different terms.

What to Ask

Staff cannot assume a tool is safe because it is popular. Ask which tool is approved, what it keeps, and whether the content is allowed to leave the university's agreements.

Personal data and confidentiality

Text, images and files entered into a tool can count as personal data under UK data protection law. Check that a tool is approved before entering student, staff or confidential material. [26] This includes names, images and free-text comments about identifiable people. If unsure, ask your data protection contact before using the tool.

A Quick Example

Pasting a student's essay with a name and a feedback comment into an unapproved tool sends personal data to a third party, which is a data protection issue before it is an academic one.

Where It Gets Tricky

Anonymising helps but may not be enough where the person is still identifiable. Treat unpublished research, assessment material and commercially sensitive material the same way.

Prompting in practice

Clear results usually come from stating the task, the audience, the constraints and what good looks like, giving an example, then iterating. Ask for sources or reasoning and check them. It is a skill that improves with practice, and it does not replace subject knowledge.

A Quick Example

"Write about branding" gives something generic; "Give three critiques of this logo for a student audience, name the strongest, and say what would change your view" gives something checkable. Iterating (asking for changes, alternatives or the opposite view) usually beats a long first prompt. Good prompting depends on knowing the subject well enough to see when the answer is wrong.

Agents (agentic AI)

Systems that carry out multi-step tasks with tools, with less step-by-step human control. They raise new questions about oversight, cost and who is accountable when something goes wrong. [9] The question of who stays accountable is picked up under human in the loop in Ways of thinking.

A Quick Example

An agent asked to book meeting rooms might read a calendar, send emails and change entries. Each action may be correct, but a wrong early step can carry through the rest, and the user may not see every step.

What to Decide in Advance

Decide in advance what it may read, what it may change, what needs a person's approval, and where its actions are logged.

Workflow, automation and augmentation

Automation means AI does a task in place of a person; augmentation means it supports a person who stays in control. A workflow is a fixed sequence of steps, set by people, in which AI handles some; an agent differs in deciding its own steps. Which of these a tool offers changes the oversight needed. [78]

A Quick Example

A fixed workflow might have AI summarise each form response before a person reads it; an agent could decide for itself which sources to search and what to do next. Automation moves the task from a person to a tool, and augmentation keeps the person responsible for judgement.

Why It Matters

Which pattern a tool follows decides who needs training, what needs checking and where accountability sits.

Deskilling and over-reliance

When a tool does the practice for you, the skill the practice was meant to build may not form. The evidence is mixed and still thin for creative work, so treat this as a question to test in your own subject, not a settled harm. [113][114]

What the Evidence Shows

In a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school maths students, those given an unrestricted chatbot did better during practice but worse than a no-AI group once it was taken away. A tutor built to give hints and not answers did not show the harm. [113] A survey of 319 knowledge workers linked higher confidence in the AI to less reported critical thinking, and higher confidence in their own ability to more. [114] These studies are of maths students and office workers, not art or design courses. A small study of six industrial designers, set beside a literature review, describes upskilling, reskilling and deskilling happening together. [115]

The Other Side

Deskilling is not automatic: the maths study found the way the tool was built changed the result. [113] An industry survey of 906 designers reports that those using AI say it improves speed and quality, but it comes from a software vendor and was read here through a secondary summary, so weigh it as one view. [117]

In a Creative Setting

Ask which skills in your subject are built by doing the slow version first, and where the process matters more than the output. One study of 34 undergraduate textile design students proposes working in exploration, refinement and reflection phases and assessing the prompt and decision history alongside the result. [116] Tension to test with subject leads: the sources describe skill loss where tools give answers, but studios may already teach students to treat a fast first output as a draft to push against. Does that hold in your subject?

What to Ask

Which skill should a student still have once the tool is taken away? Is this a task where doing it unaided is the learning?

Rules, support and who decides

Staff often meet AI first as inconsistent rules, thin training and no time. A clear institutional position says what is allowed, who decides, and what support exists, and it applies the same way across a programme. Evidence suggests many UK universities are not there yet. [118][119]

What the Evidence Shows

Studies of UK universities describe contested and uneven approaches. One study of 44 universities found 30% had a formal AI policy while 70% offered practical guidance. [119] A HEPI note on 96 institutions reported that about two in five still lacked a student-facing AI policy. [118] Students feel the unevenness inside one programme, meeting different rules and expectations from different staff. [5] The Office for Students says students are frequently unclear whether and how they may use AI in assessed work. [121] In HEPI's 2026 student survey, 37% said their institution encourages AI use and 36% said it discourages it. [120]

The Other Side

Students' own ratings of clarity vary by survey: in HEPI's 2025 survey, 80% called their institution's policy clear. [124] Treat any single figure as one reading, since the surveys differ in wording and year.

For Staff

The staff side is less studied. Jisc's 2025 staff work found staff are not adapting from a level playing field, with different resources, infrastructure and support. [4] Jisc roundtables named lack of time as the overriding barrier, with staff caught between pressures to innovate, comply and keep student trust. [122] A small analysis of staff-facing guidance at 16 universities found it much less developed than the student-facing kind. [123]

In a Creative Setting

Studio and critique cultures differ by subject, so one rule may not fit every task. Ask subject leads where rules already differ between modules in the same programme, and what that does to students. Tension to test: the sources call for one clear institutional rule, but creative subjects may need different rules for different tasks, such as concept development and final craft. Can a position be consistent and still subject-specific?

What to Ask

Who decides which tools staff may use or buy? What can a colleague do today if they are unsure? What training and protected time exist, and who owns them? Do students on the same programme get the same answer?

05 Contemporary themes

Copyright and training data

Whether AI developers may train on protected work is unsettled in the UK. The March 2026 government report did not take forward its earlier preferred training exception, and no reform is yet legislated. [2] Related questions remain open: whether outputs that resemble protected work infringe, who owns what a tool generates, and what licensing creators should expect. Rights holders and developers are also testing these points in court. [52][80][53]

A Quick Example

A designer asks whether an image made "in the style of" a named illustrator can be used commercially. Today the answer depends on the jurisdiction, the tool's terms and the facts, with no settled UK rule.

What to Do

Read a tool's licence terms for outputs, and keep a record of prompts and sources for client work.

Consent, likeness and deepfakes

Synthetic voice and image raise questions of consent and creator rights. Students report growing concern about deepfakes. [3] Voice and likeness can be cloned from short samples, so consent and compensation matter for performers, voice artists and models. [107]

A Quick Example

A short recording can be enough to synthesise speech in that person's voice, and a photo can be edited into a scene the person never agreed to.

What to Do

In teaching, get consent before using classmates' or colleagues' images or voices, and check whether a tool's terms let it keep or reuse uploads.

Provenance

Content Credentials (C2PA) attach a signed record of how an asset was made and changed. They show origin, not quality. [10] Labels can be stripped, and a missing credential does not show that something is AI-made or human-made. [81][82]

A Quick Example

Some cameras and tools add a credential when they make or edit an image, and many platforms remove it on upload.

How to Read It

Treat a present credential as a useful clue about origin and an absent one as no information. Neither tells you whether the content is accurate.

Environmental cost

The IEA projects data-centre electricity demand to more than double to around 945 TWh by 2030, with AI the biggest driver. [9] Estimates vary and efficiency is changing quickly, so treat any single figure with care.

Why Estimates Differ

Estimates differ because they count different things (one chip, a whole data centre, training as well as use), so the useful questions are: per what unit, what is included, who measured it, and compared with what? Per query the figures reported are small; the argument is about scale and about where the electricity comes from. The token calculator in this primer shows how much the estimates spread.

Creative work and labour

UK policy for the sector favours an augmentation-first approach, with AI strengthening human creativity rather than replacing it. [1] The live tension is between tools that extend a creator's reach and tools that replace commissioned work, entry-level roles or fees. [61]

A Quick Example

A studio that once bought stock illustration may now generate it, and entry-level roles that were a route into the sector may shrink. Students are training for roles that are changing.

A Question for Any Course

A useful question for any course: what skills and portfolio evidence still show a person's own judgement?

What the Sector Evidence Says

The BFI and CoSTAR screen-sector report raises legal, ethical and practical challenges including unlicensed training data, job loss and skills investment [84]; BIMA's standards put human oversight and creators' rights first [85]; Skills England's creative-industries assessment gives the skills-demand picture [83]. Adobe's survey of 16,000+ creators is vendor evidence [86]. BFI funding applications must disclose AI use [92].

Labour behind the models

Training and safety filtering rely on large amounts of human data work, often outsourced and low paid. A 2023 TIME investigation reported Kenyan workers labelling harmful content for OpenAI at under about $2 an hour; the contractor disputed some of the details. [27]

A Quick Example

Before a chatbot refuses a harmful request, people may have had to read and label examples of harmful content.

Why It Matters for Staff

The point for staff is that "the model" rests on human work that is usually invisible. Useful questions: who is paid, how much and under what conditions, and does the supplier say?

Equity and access

Tools differ in cost and capability, so students do not all start equal. Support and clear expectations matter. [3][5]

A Quick Example

Paid tiers can outperform free ones, models perform unevenly across languages, and detectors have wrongly flagged non-native English writing. [25] One student pays for a premium tool while another uses a free one with tighter limits, and a student writing in a second language may get weaker help or be wrongly flagged.

What to Do

Say which tools are supported, give access to them, and design tasks that do not depend on a paid tool.

Assessment validity

The question is no longer only "was AI used?" but whether assessment still shows that the student met the standard. [5][6] The design question is what the assessment is meant to evidence, and whether the process (drafts, decisions, reflection, conversation) can be made visible.

A Quick Example

A take-home report a tool can write in minutes may no longer show the student's own analysis, whereas a short viva or a process portfolio might.

Ways to Keep the Standard Visible

Options that keep the standard visible include in-person components, staged drafts with commentary and reflection on decisions; each has time and fairness costs. Frameworks to compare: the five-level AI Assessment Scale, piloted at one institution by its authors [91], and the QAA-funded work on making human thinking visible in assessment [90].

Regulation

The EU AI Act is in force in stages [13]; the UK uses five cross-sector principles (safety, security and robustness; transparency and explainability; fairness; accountability and governance; contestability and redress). [14]

What It Says for Education

Under the EU AI Act, Annex III treats AI used in education and vocational training as high risk where it decides admission or access, evaluates learning outcomes, assesses the level of education a person will receive, or monitors prohibited behaviour during tests. [13][97] The Digital Omnibus has since moved the application date for those stand-alone high-risk obligations from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027. [97][98]

In the UK, and for a Department

UK regulators apply the principles within existing law. For a department the practical point is to know which rules apply to the tool and to the use, and who in the institution is responsible for checking.

Staff and student literacy differ

Staff policy lags behind practice, and students want clear, fair, practical support. [3][4][7]

A Quick Example

A student may use a tool daily and know its habits better than staff, while staff know the standards and risks of the discipline.

A Useful Start

A useful start is to ask both groups what they actually do, then write guidance that matches it. A shared vocabulary, such as the one in this primer, helps conversations begin from the same place. Creative-sector examples: UAL's London College of Communication piloted an AI e-module for Moving Image and Digital Arts [89]; Falmouth frames its stance as holding space for debate rather than taking one position [87]; Arts University Bournemouth publishes a policy statement [94].

06 Ways of thinking (lenses to discuss, not settled claims)

Each is a discussion question.

  • Directing or serving? (centaur and reverse centaur) In Doctorow's terms, a centaur is a person assisted by a machine, and a reverse centaur is a person made to assist one, working at its pace and taking the blame when it fails.

    Questions to Sit with

    Do I direct this tool, and would I stand behind its output? Who answers when it is wrong, and is the "human in the loop" there to oversee or to take the blame? Elish calls the person who absorbs that blame a moral crumple zone. [15][16][17] Does supervising automation erode the supervisor's own skill? [18]

    Try It

    Think of the last time you accepted a tool's first output. Who decided?

  • Where does the judgement live? Can craft judgement be written down as rules, or is it learned only by doing? What happens to it if the doing is delegated? [19]

    What the Thinkers Say

    [20] Schön describes professionals who know more in action than they can state, and Polanyi calls this tacit knowledge.

    Try It

    Which decisions in your practice could you not explain in writing to a newcomer?

  • Whose values are in the tool? Tools carry the assumptions and interests of those who built them. What do ours assume, and who benefits? [21]

    What the Thinkers Say

    [22] Feenberg argues that design choices embody values that could have been otherwise, and Winner argues that artefacts can carry politics.

    Try It

    Whose images, words and working conditions went in, and who was asked?

  • Partner or instrument? One tradition (posthumanism) treats AI as an active participant in creative work; another treats it as an instrument of human creativity.

    Questions to Sit with

    What would it take to see it differently, and what would we gain or lose? [23]

    What the Thinkers Say

    Braidotti's posthuman thought questions the sharp line between human and non-human agents.

    Try It

    When you say "my work" about something made with a tool, what are you claiming?

  • What counts as taking? Imitation, quotation, sampling, pastiche and plagiarism sit on a spectrum, and law, ethics and academic integrity draw the lines in different places. What separates influence from theft: consent, transformation, disclosure?

    Questions to Sit with

    Where do a model's training and a student's use of its output sit? The much-quoted line that mature poets steal is Eliot's (1920), not Wilde's, and he goes on to say what matters is what is made of what is taken. [28][29]

    One Way to Start

    One way to start is to separate consent (was it licensed?), transformation (what was done with it?) and disclosure (was the source acknowledged?).

    Try It

    Ask a tool who first said a famous line, then check the answer.

  • Whose authority is this? A claim often arrives carrying a name, a number or a citation that makes it feel checked.

    Three Ways It Goes Wrong

    Sometimes the name never said it: the "insanity" line credited to Einstein has no substantive evidence behind it, and the earliest close match is a 1981 Narcotics Anonymous pamphlet. [38] Sometimes the source is real but stretched: Mehrabian's 7-38-55 figures come from two 1967 studies of single spoken words and attitudes of liking, yet are quoted as a rule for all communication. [34] Sometimes the numbers were added later: Dale's 1946 "cone of experience" had no percentages, and the retention figures attached to it trace to a 1967 article that cited no research. [35][36]

    How AI Makes It Worse

    Generative AI makes this easier to do by accident, because a fabricated reference looks like a real one: in Mata v. Avianca (2023) lawyers filed cases invented by ChatGPT and were sanctioned. [37]

    Before Passing a Claim on

    Before passing on a claim: who first said it, where, and what exactly did they claim? Does an unattributed model answer, or a confident one, carry any authority at all?

    Try It

    Pick a quotation or statistic you often repeat and trace it to the first source. Did that source say it, in that context?

07 Current models: benefits and limits (dated snapshot, changes fast)

As of 2026-11-10. Review by 2027-01-15, then every term. Specific models, scores and prices go stale in weeks, so this section describes patterns and says where to check. Treat any figure here as a dated example. Test a tool on your own task before relying on a general claim. [30][33]

What is improving fast

Capability on benchmarks is rising, not plateauing. Stanford's 2026 AI Index reports frontier models meeting or exceeding human baselines on PhD-level science questions, multimodal reasoning and competition mathematics. [30] METR measures how long a task (in human-expert time) an AI agent can complete at a given success rate. Its estimated doubling time since 2023 is about 131 days, against about 196 days over 2019-2025, though the estimate is sensitive to which tasks are measured and few long tasks are human-timed. [31]

An Illustration

Faster capability growth means any list of what a tool cannot do goes stale quickly, so guidance that names tasks ("AI cannot do X") ages worse than guidance that names checks ("verify Y before relying on it").

Benefits that hold up across models

Drafting and restructuring text, summarising material you supply, translation, code assistance, brainstorming many options quickly, explaining unfamiliar terms at the level you ask for, and turning rough notes into a first structure. Output is a first draft for a person to judge, not a finished answer. [32]

An Illustration

Pasting your own meeting notes and asking for a structured summary with action points is a task where the tool has the material and you can check the result against it, which is why these uses are lower risk than asking it for facts from memory.

What stays uneven ("jagged")

The same system can pass expert-level exams and fail simple tasks such as reading an analogue clock. [30] Good performance on a benchmark does not predict performance on your task. A 50% success horizon means it fails half of attempts at that task length. [31]

An Illustration

A tool that writes a polished analysis of a case study may still miscount the items in your table.

What to Do

Test the tool on a small sample of your own tasks, including the dull ones, before trusting it on the whole job.

Plausible but wrong output persists

Current models still produce confident errors, including invented references. [12][32] Independent benchmarks disagree with one another and measure different things (answering from memory, summarising supplied text, citing sources). Stanford's 2026 AI Index reports hallucination rates from 22% to 94% across 26 top models on one benchmark (AA-Omniscience, which tests whether a model admits it does not know). [30] Letting the tool search the web or read supplied documents lowers errors but does not remove them. [72]

An Illustration

For any factual claim you plan to repeat, find the source yourself and check it says what the tool says it does, as set out under verification in 03 Behaviour and limits.

Newer is not automatically more reliable

"reasoning" upgrades have in some tests increased certain error types while improving others: in OpenAI's own test of questions about people, o3 gave unsupported answers about twice as often as its predecessor o1. [73] Check the specific task.

Transparency is falling

The Foundation Model Transparency Index average fell from 58 to 40 out of 100, so developers disclose less about training data and limitations, which makes independent testing more important. [30]

An Illustration

If a developer does not say what a model was trained on or where it fails, a department cannot assume either; local testing and clear rules on what staff and students may enter matter more.

Agents and tool use

Tools that act on your behalf (browse, run code, edit files) extend what can be done and also what can go wrong, including errors that compound over many steps and actions that are hard to undo. [78] Ask what the tool can change and who checks it.

What does not change with versions

It predicts plausible output rather than checking truth (02 How generative AI works); it has a knowledge cutoff unless it searches (03 Behaviour and limits); responsibility for the work stays with the person who submits it; and data you paste in may leave your control (04 Using AI in practice).

How to keep this current

The questions to ask of any new model or feature are: what is it good at on my task, how would I know when it is wrong, what happens to my data, and who bears the cost of a mistake?

Where to Look

Sources to check: Jisc National Centre for AI primer [32], Stanford AI Index [30], METR [31].

An Illustration

Name an owner, keep the review date at the top, and record what changed at each review, so staff know how old the advice is and who to tell when something looks out of date.

08 AI and neurodivergence: benefits and detriments (both are real)

Neurodivergent people are not one group, and most of the evidence below is small-sample or self-reported, often from people who chose to use AI. Treat it as a set of questions to ask, and involve neurodivergent staff and students in the answers. [42][39]

Reported benefits

Starting and structuring tasks when executive function is a barrier (planning, breaking work down, getting unstuck) [39][40][41][44]; explaining or summarising dense text and suggesting ideas, where disabled students in one small UK study reported more use for these than a general-student survey [46]; a private place to ask questions without social cost, and support while diagnosis or Disabled Students' Allowance waits are long [47]; translating tone in both directions, which some autistic users say lowers the effort of masking at work. [41]

An Illustration

A student who struggles to start a report asks a tool to break the brief into steps, then does the work themselves; the help is in the structuring, not the writing.

Where It Gets Tricky

Whether a use counts as support or as doing the work depends on the task and on what the assessment is meant to show.

Where it falls short

It does not fix low motivation or time management [40]; students report inaccurate answers, subscription costs and paywalled features [39][42]; and tools are often designed for users with strong digital skills and few access needs. [42]

An Illustration

Because tools help some people and not others, guidance that assumes a tool is a universal fix, or that everyone can afford or use one, can add to the barriers it means to lower.

What to Do

Ask users what helps, rather than assuming.

Voice and masking

The same tone-translation that helps can become automated masking, with users reporting a sense that their own voice is replaced or "hollowed out". [41] Jisc raised in 2023 that tone and style tools could push students towards one standardised, neurotypical voice. [42]

An Illustration

Let students choose whether to keep their own wording, and avoid treating plain, repetitive or distinctive writing as a sign of anything.

What to Do

If a tool changes someone's tone, the person should be able to see and reverse the change.

Agreeable tools

Chatbots tuned to agree can validate hyperfixations or distorted beliefs, and emotional reliance can substitute for human contact. [41]

An Illustration

(See sycophancy in 03 Behaviour and limits.) A supportive tool can be useful for rehearsal, but it is not a substitute for human support, and it may agree with ideas a person would be better to test with someone.

What to Do

Guidance can point to human support services alongside any tool advice.

Detectors and suspicion

Detectors are documented to misflag non-native English writers [25]. A preprint reports higher misclassification of autistic writing by older GPT-2-era detectors [45]; A search in November 2026 found no peer-reviewed false-positive rate for neurodivergent writers, so treat reports of one as unconfirmed.

Why It Matters

The mechanism is plausible: plain, regular or unusual styles can look "machine-like". Style alone is not evidence of AI use (03 Behaviour and limits, detectors).

Assessment rules can remove existing support

Tools provided through the Disabled Students' Allowance (e.g. grammar and reading tools) have gained generative features. A blanket "no AI" rule can take away support students already rely on, and policies that say "AI" when they mean "generative AI" make this worse. [42]

An Illustration

Write "generative AI" where that is what is meant, list supported assistive tools as permitted, and say how a student can ask for an adjustment.

What to Do

Check wording with disability services before it is published.

Disclosure and data

Students weigh the risks of telling AI educational tools about a diagnosis, including privacy and being treated differently. [43] Do not paste health or support-plan details into general tools (04 Using AI in practice, personal data).

An Illustration

Guidance could say that no one is required to disclose a diagnosis in order to use an approved tool or to be treated fairly, and that support-plan details do not belong in general-purpose tools.

Open questions for the group

When is generative AI use a reasonable adjustment, and who decides? [42][44] How do we separate assistive use from generative use in our assessment wording? What counts as the student's own voice? Who is in the room when we write the position statement?

A Workable Start

A workable start is to ask neurodivergent staff and students to review the wording before it is agreed, and to record where their views differ.

09 By subject area (starter pattern: to be co-written with each subject's leads)

Each subject brings its own opportunities and challenges, and the evidence differs by discipline. These blocks are a starting pattern from published sources, not settled positions. Subject leads should correct and extend them, and the evidence is thin in places. Each block gives opportunities, challenges and questions to ask.

Creative writing

Opportunities

Help with ideas, structure and editing. In a small trial, guided instruction on when and how to use ChatGPT improved outcomes, especially for students who scored low on creativity unaided. [65]

Challenges

In a 225-student experiment, ChatGPT raised performance but diminished students' experience of creativity [49]. Society of Authors members raised worries about creative atrophy and losing the struggle that builds skill [51]. Authorship norms are strict, so passing machine text off as your own is read as a breach of the discipline's own values [48]. In the Society of Authors' 2024 survey, 36% of translators and 26% of illustrators had already lost work to generative AI, and 86% of respondents worried it devalues human-made creative work [50]. In the US, a judge found that training on lawfully acquired books was fair use but acquiring pirated copies was not, and a $1.5 billion settlement was approved in July 2026 (US law; the UK position is unsettled, see 05 Contemporary themes) [52].

Questions to Ask

What is the learning in the blank page, and which AI uses protect it? What must students disclose? How does workshop critique work if any text may be machine-made? (Assistive use: 08 AI and neurodivergence.)

Fine art

Opportunities

AI as material, subject or foil for critical practice (starter wording for the subject lead to confirm). Practitioners report most use early, at ideation. [61]

Challenges

Style mimicry and consent. Ten artists' copyright claims against image-generator companies were allowed to proceed in the US in 2024 [53]. The UK High Court's November 2025 ruling in Getty v Stability AI was mixed, and tools such as Glaze protect new images but cannot remove work already ingested [54]. A 2026 UAL symposium programme quotes a student describing work students cannot fully explain, under AI policies that do not work in practice [55]. Assessment in art, design and creative disciplines needs rethinking [56].

Questions to Ask

What counts as the student's own hand, process and evidence? Could in-person formats such as studio visits assess work in progress (a proposal in one Chelsea BA Fine Art revalidation) [57], and could assessment make thinking visible [66]? Whose work is being taken as input (06 Ways of thinking, "What counts as taking?")?

Fashion

Opportunities

Faster visualisation and campaign production (starter wording for the subject lead to confirm). H&M's digital-twin scheme gave 30 models ownership of their twins, payment under their agents' rates, and watermarked, disclosed images. [58]

Challenges

Hair and make-up artists, photographers and stylists have voiced worry about their work [59]. Who owns AI-assisted designs is unclear, and a UK analysis asks when assisted designs are original and when outputs could infringe [60]. Generative tools may narrow the variety of what a group produces: a 2024 experiment found AI-assisted short stories more similar to each other [104]; whether trend tools do the same is not tested here.

Questions to Ask

Who consents to, and is paid for, a likeness or a style? How is AI use disclosed in portfolios and placements? How do the roles students are training for change?

Graphic design

Opportunities

AI is used mostly at the front of the workflow, in brainstorming and ideation [61]. In Adobe's 2024 survey of 2,002 creatives, 90% said generative AI can help create new ideas, and some tools (Adobe Firefly) say they train only on licensed content, a vendor claim to test [62].

Challenges

56% of those creatives thought it can harm creators, mainly through training on work without consent [62]. In a US survey of 400 designers, 68% were reluctant to let AI make final design decisions [63]. Lancaster authors argue the effects are especially acute in design because practice is generative and open to outside ideas [64]. In the US, protection of AI output depends on human authorship, and the Copyright Office judged prompting alone insufficient [79]. Check UK and EU positions separately.

Questions to Ask

Which tools and licences are acceptable for client work, and who tells the client? Where does the designer's value sit once production is faster [61]? How do we assess judgement and decisions rather than outputs?

Architecture

Opportunities

To be written with the subject lead.

Challenges

To be written with the subject lead.

Questions to Ask (starter Prompts, to Be Replaced)

What counts as the student's own design thinking and evidence? Which tools and licences are acceptable for client or site work, and who tells the client? How is judgement assessed when drawings and renders can be generated quickly?

Games design

Opportunities

To be written with the subject lead.

Challenges

To be written with the subject lead.

Questions to Ask (starter Prompts, to Be Replaced)

What counts as the student's own design, code, art and writing? How are generated assets handled in a team project, and what must be disclosed? What do studios and the industry expect of graduates?

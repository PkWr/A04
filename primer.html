AI literacy primer for staff

This primer is for SCII staff, whatever your subject or starting point. It helps you understand the main ideas in plain language, judge claims, tools and sources for yourself, see where your own subject's opportunities and challenges differ, and treat contested ideas as questions to discuss. Use the side bar to find a place to start by asking a question, or read the tiers in order. Numbers in square brackets link to the numbered references at the end.

Draft for review. Not approved, not for circulation. Built from the markdown draft on 16 November 2026.

1. The basics

Artificial intelligence (AI)
software that performs tasks we normally associate with human intelligence, such as recognising, predicting, translating or recommending. Once something works reliably we stop calling it AI (spell-check). In current news, "AI" usually means generative AI, but the term covers much older tools too. [7]
Rule-based automation
people write the rules ("if invoice over £10,000, send for approval"). Predictable and auditable, but breaks on cases nobody anticipated. A useful contrast: a rule-based system fails in ways someone can list; a learned system can fail in ways nobody listed.
Machine learning
the system learns patterns from examples instead of following written rules. In practice that means adjusting a very large set of numbers until the outputs fit the examples. Nobody writes the rules it ends up using, and they cannot simply be read off. The output of that learning is a model.
Model (parameters, weights)
the product of training: a very large set of numbers that encodes patterns found in the training data. It is not a searchable store of the original material, though it can sometimes reproduce parts of it. [src?]
Training data
the examples a model learned from, which may include text, images, code and audio, some licensed and some collected from the web. Its coverage, gaps and biases shape what the model can do, and whether the people who made the material consented to its use is a live question. [8][2]
Training and inference
training builds the model once, at great cost; inference is each time someone uses it. Both use energy, and the balance between them shapes the environmental picture. [9]

2. How generative AI works

Generative AI
models that produce new text, images, audio, video or code, rather than only classifying or predicting. The output is usually newly assembled from patterns in the training data rather than retrieved from a stored copy, and quality varies a lot by task. [7]
Large language model (LLM)
a model trained on very large amounts of text to predict what comes next. Later training on instructions and human feedback shapes it to follow requests and sound helpful. It produces plausible language, not checked facts. [11][src?]
Token
the small chunk of text (a word or part of a word) that a language model reads and writes. Limits, costs and some odd failures, such as miscounting letters in a word, come from the model working in tokens rather than letters. [src?]

Why the range is so wide

  • Boundaries differ: chips only, the whole serving stack, or a full lifecycle including training and hardware. Google's own paper says inconsistent boundaries drive most of the spread.
  • Model size matters: Mistral reports impact roughly in proportion to model size for the same number of tokens.
  • Reasoning and long inputs cost more: a reasoning model writes many hidden tokens, and a long document in the prompt costs more than a short one.
  • Per-token figures here are scaled from per-response figures (about 500 tokens a response, 400 for Mistral), so they are rough. Where a source gives no token count we assumed 500.

Not included

Your own device and network, and (in most frameworks) training. Some sources report only energy, water or carbon, so each range uses only the frameworks that report it.

Prompt and context window
the instructions you give, and how much text the model can consider at once. Anything outside the window is not seen, so long conversations can drift or lose early instructions. What you paste in is processed by the provider: see personal data below.
Diffusion model
the usual approach for image generation: it refines random noise into an image that fits the prompt. The same prompt gives a different image each time, and arguments about imitating artists' styles and training on their work are most visible here. [2]
Multimodal
handles more than one kind of input or output, such as text and images together, so a tool can describe an image, or generate one from a sketch and a sentence.
Fine-tuning and retrieval
two ways of adapting a general model. Fine-tuning trains it further on specialist examples; retrieval gives it documents to draw on when answering. Retrieval can reduce errors but does not remove them. [src?]

3. Behaviour and limits

Probabilistic output
the model assigns a probability to each possible next word and the tool samples from those, rather than always taking the top choice. A setting often called temperature controls how adventurous the sampling is. So the same prompt can give different answers, and a good result once is not a guarantee. Updates, hidden system prompts and earlier conversation also change results. For assessment, a marker cannot reproduce what a student's tool produced. [src?]
Hallucination
fluent output that is false or unsupported, including invented references, quotations and dates. There is no intent to deceive, and a confident tone is not evidence. Risk is higher where the topic is obscure or the request asks for specific sources. [12][src?]
Bias
patterns in training data can be reproduced or amplified in outputs, for example stereotyped images, uneven performance across languages and dialects, or a narrow idea of the "default" user. Detection tools can show it too. [8][11][25]
Verification
checking claims against sources you trust. Check that any cited source exists and says what is claimed; check images and quotations for provenance. It is the user's job, not the tool's.
AI detectors
tools that claim to flag AI-written text. Jisc advises that none can conclusively prove AI authorship, all give false positives, and they are easy to defeat; one study found them wrongly flagging non-native English writing. Treat a score as a prompt for a conversation, not as evidence. [24][25]
Agreeing too readily (sycophancy)
tools tend to go along with the framing of a question and to praise the user's work, so a leading prompt can get a confirming answer. Ask for counter-arguments and test answers against your own judgement. [src?]
Knowledge cutoff and currency
a model knows only what was in its training data up to a certain date. Tools that search the web can add current information, but quality varies, so check dates and sources.
Explanations are not readouts
if you ask a model why it gave an answer, the reply is another generated text, not a record of how it actually worked. Treat self-explanations with the same care as any other output. [src?]

4. Using AI in practice

AI-assisted vs AI-generated
how much of the thinking and the work is yours. The range runs from brainstorming and editing, through drafting with heavy revision, to submitting generated work unchanged. The difference matters for authorship, assessment and credit, and for who is accountable for the result.
Disclosure
saying when and how AI was used. It is about transparency and trust as well as rule-following. Expectations differ between modules, so clarity matters more than the rule itself. [3][5]
Supported tools and data boundary
use institutional accounts for university work, so prompts stay inside the university's data protection arrangements. (Copilot is the SHU supported tool; see the Copilot Susceptibility Test Protocol.) Free consumer tools may handle inputs differently depending on their settings and terms, so check before use. [src?]
Personal data and confidentiality
text, images and files entered into a tool can count as personal data under UK data protection law. Check that a tool is approved before entering student, staff or confidential material. [26] This includes names, images and free-text comments about identifiable people. If unsure, ask your data protection contact before using the tool.
Prompting in practice
clear results usually come from stating the task, the audience, the constraints and what good looks like, giving an example, then iterating. Ask for sources or reasoning and check them. It is a skill that improves with practice, and it does not replace subject knowledge. [src?]
Agents (agentic AI)
systems that carry out multi-step tasks with tools, with less step-by-step human control. They raise new questions about oversight, cost and who is accountable when something goes wrong. [9]

5. Contemporary themes

Copyright and training data
whether AI developers may train on protected work is unsettled in the UK. The March 2026 government report did not take forward its earlier preferred training exception, and no reform is yet legislated. [2] Related questions remain open: whether outputs that resemble protected work infringe, who owns what a tool generates, and what licensing creators should expect. Rights holders and developers are also testing these points in court. [src?]
Consent, likeness and deepfakes
synthetic voice and image raise questions of consent and creator rights. Students report growing concern about deepfakes. [3] Voice and likeness can be cloned from short samples, so consent and compensation matter for performers, voice artists and models. [src?]
Provenance
Content Credentials (C2PA) attach a signed record of how an asset was made and changed. They show origin, not quality. [10] Labels can be stripped, and a missing credential does not show that something is AI-made or human-made. [src?]
Environmental cost
the IEA projects data-centre electricity demand to more than double to around 945 TWh by 2030, with AI the biggest driver. [9] Estimates vary and efficiency is changing quickly, so treat any single figure with care.
Creative work and labour
UK policy for the sector favours an augmentation-first approach, with AI strengthening human creativity rather than replacing it. [1] The live tension is between tools that extend a creator's reach and tools that replace commissioned work, entry-level roles or fees. [src?]
Labour behind the models
training and safety filtering rely on large amounts of human data work, often outsourced and low paid. A 2023 TIME investigation reported Kenyan workers labelling harmful content for OpenAI at under about $2 an hour; the contractor disputed some of the details. [27]
Equity and access
tools differ in cost and capability, so students do not all start equal. Support and clear expectations matter. [3][5] Examples: paid tiers can outperform free ones, models perform unevenly across languages, and detectors have wrongly flagged non-native English writing. [25]
Assessment validity
the question is no longer only "was AI used?" but whether assessment still shows that the student met the standard. [5][6] The design question is what the assessment is meant to evidence, and whether the process (drafts, decisions, reflection, conversation) can be made visible.
Regulation
the EU AI Act is in force in stages [13]; the UK uses five cross-sector principles (safety, security and robustness; transparency and explainability; fairness; accountability and governance; contestability and redress). [14]
Staff and student literacy differ
staff policy lags behind practice, and students want clear, fair, practical support. [3][4][7]

6. Ways of thinking (lenses to discuss, not settled claims)

Each is a discussion question.

  • Directing or serving? (centaur and reverse centaur) In Doctorow's terms, a centaur is a person assisted by a machine, and a reverse centaur is a person made to assist one, working at its pace and taking the blame when it fails. Do I direct this tool, and would I stand behind its output? Who answers when it is wrong, and is the "human in the loop" there to oversee or to take the blame? Elish calls the person who absorbs that blame a moral crumple zone. [15][16][17] Does supervising automation erode the supervisor's own skill? [18] Try it: think of the last time you accepted a tool's first output. Who decided?
  • Where does the judgement live? Can craft judgement be written down as rules, or is it learned only by doing? What happens to it if the doing is delegated? [19] [20] Schön describes professionals who know more in action than they can state, and Polanyi calls this tacit knowledge. Try it: which decisions in your practice could you not explain in writing to a newcomer?
  • Whose values are in the tool? Tools carry the assumptions and interests of those who built them. What do ours assume, and who benefits? [21] [22] Feenberg argues that design choices embody values that could have been otherwise, and Winner argues that artefacts can carry politics. Try it: whose images, words and working conditions went in, and who was asked?
  • Partner or instrument? One tradition (posthumanism) treats AI as an active participant in creative work; another treats it as an instrument of human creativity. What would it take to argue otherwise, and what would we lose? [23] Braidotti's posthuman thought questions the sharp line between human and non-human agents. Try it: when you say "my work" about something made with a tool, what are you claiming?
  • What counts as taking? Imitation, quotation, sampling, pastiche and plagiarism sit on a spectrum, and law, ethics and academic integrity draw the lines in different places. What separates influence from theft: consent, transformation, disclosure? Where do a model's training and a student's use of its output sit? The much-quoted line that mature poets steal is Eliot's (1920), not Wilde's, and he goes on to say what matters is what is made of what is taken. [28][29] One way to start is to separate consent (was it licensed?), transformation (what was done with it?) and disclosure (was the source acknowledged?). Try it: ask a tool who first said a famous line, then check the answer.
  • Whose authority is this? A claim often arrives carrying a name, a number or a citation that makes it feel checked. Sometimes the name never said it: the "insanity" line credited to Einstein has no substantive evidence behind it, and the earliest close match is a 1981 Narcotics Anonymous pamphlet. [38] Sometimes the source is real but stretched: Mehrabian's 7-38-55 figures come from two 1967 studies of single spoken words and attitudes of liking, yet are quoted as a rule for all communication. [34] Sometimes the numbers were added later: Dale's 1946 "cone of experience" had no percentages, and the retention figures attached to it trace to a 1967 article that cited no research. [35][36] Generative AI makes this easier to do by accident, because a fabricated reference looks like a real one: in Mata v. Avianca (2023) lawyers filed cases invented by ChatGPT and were sanctioned. [37] Before passing on a claim: who first said it, where, and what exactly did they claim? Does an unattributed model answer, or a confident one, carry any authority at all?

7. Current models: benefits and limits (dated snapshot, changes fast)

As of 2026-11-10. Review by 2027-01-15, then every term. Specific models, scores and prices go stale in weeks, so this section describes patterns and says where to check. Treat any figure here as a dated example. Test a tool on your own task before relying on a general claim. [30][33]

What is improving fast
capability on benchmarks is rising, not plateauing. Stanford's 2026 AI Index reports frontier models meeting or exceeding human baselines on PhD-level science questions, multimodal reasoning and competition mathematics. [30] METR measures how long a task (in human-expert time) an AI agent can complete at a given success rate. Its estimated doubling time since 2023 is about 131 days, against about 196 days over 2019-2025, though the estimate is sensitive to which tasks are measured and few long tasks are human-timed. [31]
Benefits that hold up across models
drafting and restructuring text, summarising material you supply, translation, code assistance, brainstorming many options quickly, explaining unfamiliar terms at the level you ask for, and turning rough notes into a first structure. Output is a first draft for a person to judge, not a finished answer. [32]
What stays uneven ("jagged")
the same system can pass expert-level exams and fail simple tasks such as reading an analogue clock. [30] Good performance on a benchmark does not predict performance on your task. A 50% success horizon means it fails half of attempts at that task length. [31]
Plausible but wrong output persists
current models still produce confident errors, including invented references. [12][32] Independent benchmarks disagree with one another and measure different things (answering from memory, summarising supplied text, citing sources). One roundup of 26 top models reports hallucination rates from 22% to 94% on one knowledge benchmark. [src?] Letting the tool search the web or read supplied documents lowers errors but does not remove them. [src?]
Newer is not automatically more reliable
"reasoning" upgrades have in some tests increased certain error types while improving others. [src?] Check the specific task.
Transparency is falling
the Foundation Model Transparency Index average fell from 58 to 40 out of 100, so developers disclose less about training data and limitations, which makes independent testing more important. [30]
Agents and tool use
tools that act on your behalf (browse, run code, edit files) extend what can be done and also what can go wrong, including errors that compound over many steps and actions that are hard to undo. [src?] Ask what the tool can change and who checks it.
What does not change with versions
it predicts plausible output rather than checking truth (tier 2); it has a knowledge cutoff unless it searches (tier 3); responsibility for the work stays with the person who submits it; and data you paste in may leave your control (tier 5).
How to keep this current
the questions to ask of any new model or feature are: what is it good at on my task, how would I know when it is wrong, what happens to my data, and who bears the cost of a mistake? Sources to check: Jisc National Centre for AI primer [32], Stanford AI Index [30], METR [31].

8. AI and neurodivergence: benefits and detriments (both are real)

Neurodivergent people are not one group, and most of the evidence below is small-sample or self-reported, often from people who chose to use AI. Treat it as a set of questions to ask, and involve neurodivergent staff and students in the answers. [42][39]

Reported benefits
starting and structuring tasks when executive function is a barrier (planning, breaking work down, getting unstuck) [39][40][41][44]; explaining or summarising dense text and suggesting ideas, where disabled students in one small UK study reported more use for these than a general-student survey [46]; a private place to ask questions without social cost, and support while diagnosis or Disabled Students' Allowance waits are long [47]; translating tone in both directions, which some autistic users say lowers the effort of masking at work. [41]
Where it falls short
it does not fix low motivation or time management [40]; students report inaccurate answers, subscription costs and paywalled features [39][42]; and tools are often designed for users with strong digital skills and few access needs. [42]
Voice and masking
the same tone-translation that helps can become automated masking, with users reporting a sense that their own voice is replaced or "hollowed out". [41] Jisc raised in 2023 that tone and style tools could push students towards one standardised, neurotypical voice. [42]
Agreeable tools
chatbots tuned to agree can validate hyperfixations or distorted beliefs, and emotional reliance can substitute for human contact. [41] (See sycophancy, tier 3.)
Detectors and suspicion
detectors are documented to misflag non-native English writers [25]. A preprint reports higher misclassification of autistic writing by older GPT-2-era detectors [45]; I found no peer-reviewed false-positive rate for neurodivergent writers, so treat reports of one as unconfirmed. [src?] The mechanism is plausible: plain, regular or unusual styles can look "machine-like". Style alone is not evidence of AI use (tier 4, detectors).
Assessment rules can remove existing support
tools provided through the Disabled Students' Allowance (e.g. grammar and reading tools) have gained generative features. A blanket "no AI" rule can take away support students already rely on, and policies that say "AI" when they mean "generative AI" make this worse. [42]
Disclosure and data
students weigh the risks of telling AI educational tools about a diagnosis, including privacy and being treated differently. [43] Do not paste health or support-plan details into general tools (tier 5, personal data).
Open questions for the group
when is generative AI use a reasonable adjustment, and who decides? [42][44] How do we separate assistive use from generative use in our assessment wording? What counts as the student's own voice? Who is in the room when we write the position statement?

9. By subject area (starter pattern: to be co-written with each subject's leads)

Each subject brings its own opportunities and challenges, and the evidence differs by discipline. These blocks are a starting pattern from published sources, not settled positions. Subject leads should correct and extend them, and the evidence is thin in places. Each block gives opportunities, challenges and questions to ask.

Creative writing
Opportunities: help with ideas, structure and editing. In a small trial, guided instruction on when and how to use ChatGPT improved outcomes, especially for students who scored low on creativity unaided. [65] Challenges: in a 225-student experiment, ChatGPT raised performance but diminished students' experience of creativity [49]. Society of Authors members raised worries about creative atrophy and losing the struggle that builds skill [51]. Authorship norms are strict, so passing machine text off as your own is read as a breach of the discipline's own values [48]. In the Society of Authors' 2024 survey, 36% of translators and 26% of illustrators had already lost work to generative AI, and 86% of respondents worried it devalues human-made creative work [50]. In the US, a judge found that training on lawfully acquired books was fair use but acquiring pirated copies was not, and a $1.5 billion settlement was approved in July 2026 (US law; the UK position is unsettled, see tier 5) [52]. Questions to ask: What is the learning in the blank page, and which AI uses protect it? What must students disclose? How does workshop critique work if any text may be machine-made? (Assistive use: tier 8.)
Fine art
Opportunities: AI as material, subject or foil for critical practice [src?]. Practitioners report most use early, at ideation. [61] Challenges: style mimicry and consent. Ten artists' copyright claims against image-generator companies were allowed to proceed in the US in 2024 [53]. The UK High Court's November 2025 ruling in Getty v Stability AI was mixed, and tools such as Glaze protect new images but cannot remove work already ingested [54]. A 2026 UAL symposium programme quotes a student describing work students cannot fully explain, under AI policies that do not work in practice [55]. Assessment in art, design and creative disciplines needs rethinking [56]. Questions to ask: What counts as the student's own hand, process and evidence? Could in-person formats such as studio visits assess work in progress (a proposal in one Chelsea BA Fine Art revalidation) [57], and could assessment make thinking visible [66]? Whose work is being taken as input (tier 6, "What counts as taking?")?
Fashion
Opportunities: faster visualisation and campaign production [src?]. H&M's digital-twin scheme gave 30 models ownership of their twins, payment under their agents' rates, and watermarked, disclosed images. [58] Challenges: hair and make-up artists, photographers and stylists have voiced worry about their work [59]. Who owns AI-assisted designs is unclear, and a UK analysis asks when assisted designs are original and when outputs could infringe [60]. Trend tools trained on past data may narrow novelty [src?]. Questions to ask: Who consents to, and is paid for, a likeness or a style? How is AI use disclosed in portfolios and placements? How do the roles students are training for change? [src?]
Graphic design
Opportunities: AI is used mostly at the front of the workflow, in brainstorming and ideation [61]. In Adobe's 2024 survey of 2,002 creatives, 90% said generative AI can help create new ideas, and some tools (Adobe Firefly) say they train only on licensed content, a vendor claim to test [62]. Challenges: 56% of those creatives thought it can harm creators, mainly through training on work without consent [62]. In a US survey of 400 designers, 68% were reluctant to let AI make final design decisions [63]. Lancaster authors argue the effects are especially acute in design because practice is generative and open to outside ideas [64]. Protection of AI output depends on human authorship in the US and is under review in the UK and EU [src?]. Questions to ask: Which tools and licences are acceptable for client work, and who tells the client? Where does the designer's value sit once production is faster [61]? How do we assess judgement and decisions rather than outputs?
More subjects
add others with their leads, using the same three headings: opportunities, challenges, questions to ask.
